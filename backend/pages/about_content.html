iLit: immigration litigation intelligence

iLit is a research tool for Canadian immigration case law that I am building independently, on public decisions only. It lets you search the library, read a decision with its citations and statute references alongside the text, and see how a judge, an authority or the Federal Court docket fits into the wider picture. This page describes what works today and what does not yet.

What is in the library

Decisions from four courts and tribunals, each kept as published with its source address, and split into numbered paragraphs so a result can point to a passage. The counts below were taken from the database on 6 October.

Court or tribunalDecisionsYears covered
Federal Court (FC)35,9812001–2026
Supreme Court of Canada (SCC)10,8931877–2026
Federal Court of Appeal (FCA)7,8132001–2026
Immigration and Refugee Board, Refugee Protection Division (RPD)6,7292002–2020

A further 45 older records carry no decision text. The library is not a complete archive of any of these courts. Immigration Division and Immigration Appeal Division decisions are not in it yet.

Alongside the decisions, the library holds a separate record of the Federal Court docket for immigration files: the dated entries recorded on each file, such as filing, leave and judgment. It is a different kind of record from a decision, and it is what the Federal Court Analytics view draws on.

LiveResearch

Search runs entirely on the stored library. Nothing you type is sent to an AI service.

  • Find a case. Type a case name, party, citation or phrase. The search looks at names and citations and at the reasons in the decisions themselves. A short plain-words line under the box says what the search is doing.
  • See why it matched. Each result shows an excerpt with your words highlighted, the court, date, outcome and how many other decisions cite it.
  • Narrow it down. Advanced filters cover court, decision year, decision result, judge, Minister or government party, a tag, and a case that the results must cite. Court and year chips narrow results in one click, and results can be sorted by newest, oldest, relevance or most cited.
  • Take results with you. A Copy citation button sits on results and in the reader, and a search can be downloaded as a spreadsheet or Word file.

LiveThe case reader

Open any result to read the decision in a clean formatted view built from the original text.

  • Readable layout. Headings, numbered paragraphs, quotations and signature blocks are set out by fixed rules rather than shown as one block of text. A header row gives the court, citation, case name, file number, decision date, decision maker and outcome, with links to the source.
  • Citations and statutes in the text. Citations to other cases and references to statutes are highlighted in place. Hovering over a pinpointed citation shows the paragraph it points to, when that case is in the library, and hovering over a statute reference shows the provision text where it is stored. A citation that cannot be matched says so rather than guessing.
  • Around the text. A side panel gives the case information and the authorities and statutes the decision refers to, and there is a search box for the text of the decision itself. A chunk-by-chunk view of the same text is also available.
  • Outcomes say when they are unsure. The outcome shown comes from fixed rules read against the decision. If the rules cannot tell, it says “unclear” instead of picking one.

Some reader tools are still unfinished, so they stay hidden unless you switch on “Show experimental” at the top of the reader.

LiveIntelligence / Statistics

Three views that add up many decisions.

Judge Profiles

A judge’s decisions in the library, outcomes by issue with the full count shown (unclassified decisions included), Federal Court docket activity, and a side-by-side comparison of two judges. Judge names in the source decisions are written many ways, so duplicate profiles still exist while the cleanup is finished. Read these as descriptions, not rankings.

Citation Intelligence

Pick one case and see which decisions cite it, how that has changed over time, the courts and outcomes of the citing decisions, and the passages where it is cited.

Federal Court Analytics

Charts and tables over the Federal Court docket layer: how files progress, leave and judicial review outcomes by year filed, the time between steps, motions by type, judges, counsel and the registry. The docket is typed by Registry staff, so any figure inherits their errors.

How it works, and what I do not claim

  • No AI is needed to use the site. Search, the reader and the statistics read data that was prepared in advance. What you type and any file you open is not sent to any AI service. AI-related features exist in the code but are switched off by default.
  • The original comes first. Each decision is stored as published, and everything shown beside it (outcome, case information, paragraph links, labels) is derived from it by rules and kept as a separate layer. Where a fact cannot be found, the page says so.
  • It is a research aid, not legal advice. Automated reading makes mistakes. Check anything that matters against the source decision, which every result links to.
  • Public data only. Everything in the library comes from public sources. The site currently runs on my own workstation.
  • Source terms. Some source data comes with its own terms of use, which would need to be confirmed before any wider deployment.
Judge statistics are sensitive. Figures about individual judges can be misread, so the judge views show full denominators and avoid ranking.
