How it works

iLit was built by one person, with AI-assisted development used to write and check the code. This page explains where the decisions come from, how the information shown beside each one is worked out, what is live today and what is still being built.

From a published decision to a page you can search

  • Collected from public sources. Decisions are gathered from the public websites of the courts and tribunals. Each one is kept as published, with its source address, so you can always check the original.
  • Split into numbered paragraphs. This is what lets a search result, a citation or a statute reference point at a passage and not just at a whole decision.
  • Read by fixed rules. Computer programs following written rules pick out the citations to other cases, the statute and regulation references, the judge, the outcome and the type of case. The same decision always gives the same answer, and no AI is called when you use the site.
  • Kept as a separate layer. What the rules find is stored beside the original text and never replaces it. Where a fact cannot be found, the page says so rather than guessing.
  • Shown by the site. Search, the reader and the statistics read what was stored in advance. What you type, and any file you open, is not sent to an AI service.

What is in the library

Immigration decisions from four courts and tribunals. The total is shown on the Overview tab and is read from the database when the page opens.

Federal Court (FC)2001–2026The largest part of the library. Judicial reviews of immigration decisions.
Federal Court of Appeal (FCA)2001–2026Appeals from the Federal Court.
Supreme Court of Canada (SCC)1877–2026The leading authorities that the lower courts cite.
Refugee Protection Division (RPD)2002–2020Refugee claim decisions of the Immigration and Refugee Board. Later years are being added.

The library is not a complete archive of any of these courts. Immigration Division and Immigration Appeal Division decisions are not in it, because there is no open source for them yet.

Alongside the decisions, the library holds a separate record of the Federal Court docket for immigration files: the dated entries recorded on each file, such as filing, leave and judgment. It is a different kind of record from a decision, and it is what the Federal Court Analytics view draws on.

How each piece of information is worked out

Citations

References such as “2008 SCC 9” or “Vavilov at para 99” are found by pattern, matched to the decision they name when it is in the library, and linked to the paragraph cited. A citation that cannot be matched says so.

Statutes

References to Acts and regulations are found by pattern and matched to a stored library of federal statutes, so a hover can show the provision text. Matching is still being improved, so treat a statute link as a pointer, not a guarantee.

Outcomes

The outcome comes from fixed rules read against the wording of the decision. If the rules cannot tell, the page says “unclear” instead of picking one. Outcome accuracy is still being checked against a hand-reviewed set of decisions.

Case types

A label for the kind of case, for example a judicial review of a refugee decision, is worked out by rules from the decision’s own text. The label shows in the reader and can be used as a search filter.

Judges

Judge names are taken from the decisions. They are written many different ways, so a name list is used to merge the spellings. Duplicate or missing profiles still exist.

Statistics

Judge Profiles, Citation Intelligence and Federal Court Analytics add up the stored results, and show the full count so that unclassified decisions are visible. They describe the library; they do not predict a result.

What I do not claim

  • It is a research aid, not legal advice. Automated reading makes mistakes. Check anything that matters against the source decision, which every result links to.
  • No accuracy figure is promised. Each automated step is tested against decisions I have read myself, and the weak ones are listed above.
  • Public data only. Everything in the library comes from public sources. Files you open in the tools are not stored.
  • One workstation. The site and its database currently run on my own computer, so it can be briefly unavailable while it is updated. Moving it to Canadian hosting is the longer-term plan.
  • Source terms. Some source data comes with its own terms of use, which would need to be confirmed before any wider deployment.
Judge statistics are sensitive. Figures about individual judges can be misread, so the judge views show full counts and avoid ranking.
