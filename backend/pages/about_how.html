Step 1 · The short version

A website, a computer and a database

iLit is a website. The program behind it runs on one personal computer, my own, and that same computer holds a database: a single, organised store where every decision and every detail taken from it is kept.

When you open a page or run a search, your browser sends the request over the internet through a secure connection service, so the computer itself is never exposed. The website looks the answer up in the database and sends the page back. That is the whole round trip: no outside service is asked, and no AI is involved.

Step 2 · What the database holds

Decisions, court records and laws

The database holds three kinds of public information, and the details worked out from them.

Decisions

The full text of court and tribunal decisions, kept exactly as published, each with the address of its original.

Court records

The Federal Court’s public record for immigration cases: the dated entries on each case, such as filing, leave, hearing and judgment.

Laws

Federal immigration statutes and regulations, section by section, so a section cited in a decision can be read in place.

How the three are joined

A decision names its Federal Court file number and cites earlier cases and sections of laws. iLit uses those to join the three kinds of information, so you can move from a decision to its court record, or to the authority it relies on, in one click.

Step 3 · How a decision gets in

From a public source to a page you can search

  • Collected from public sources. Decisions come from the courts’ and the Board’s own public websites and from open research datasets that republish them. Each one keeps its source address, so you can always check the original.
  • Kept as published. The original text is stored once and never edited. Everything shown beside it is stored separately.
  • Split into numbered paragraphs. This lets a search result, a citation or a law reference point to one passage and not just to the whole decision.
  • Read by fixed rules. Small programs following written rules pick out the details explained in step 4: citations, laws, judge, outcome and type of case.
  • Linked to the rest of the library. A citation is matched to the decision it names when that decision is in the library, down to the paragraph cited.

Keeping the library current

New decisions and new court record entries appear every day. A daily intake job is built to find them, add only what is new or changed, and run the same steps on each. Today it is started by hand; it does not yet run automatically.

Step 4 · How the details are worked out

Fixed rules, the same answer every time

Every detail shown beside a decision comes from written rules applied to its text, not from an AI model. Run the same decision through again and you get the same result. Where the rules cannot tell, the page says so instead of guessing.

Citations

References such as “2008 SCC 9” or “Vavilov at para 99” are found by pattern, matched to the decision they name when it is in the library, and linked to the paragraph cited. A citation that cannot be matched says so.

Laws

References to Acts and regulations are found by pattern and matched to the stored federal laws, so the section can be shown in place. Matching is still being improved, so treat a law link as a pointer, not a guarantee.

Outcomes

Who won is read from the wording of the decision. If the rules cannot tell, the page says “unclear”. Outcomes are being checked against a set of decisions I reviewed by hand.

Case types

A label for the kind of case, for example a judicial review of a refugee decision, is worked out from the decision’s own text. It shows in the reader and can be used to filter a search.

Judges

Judge names are taken from the decisions. They are written many ways, so a name list merges the spellings. Some duplicate or missing names remain.

Statistics

Judge Profiles, Citation Intelligence and Federal Court Analytics add up these stored details and show the full count, so unclassified decisions stay visible. They describe the library; they do not predict a result.

How a citation becomes useful

Following a Federal Court case

The Court’s record of each immigration case is free text typed by Registry staff. Fixed rules turn those entries into steps, such as leave granted or judicial review dismissed, in English and French. Any typing error in the record carries through.

Step 5 · What happens when you use it

Everything reads what is already stored

Search, the reader and the statistics only read details that were worked out in advance. Nothing you type, and no file you open, is sent to an AI service.

  • Research. Type a case name, party, citation or phrase. The search looks at names, citations and the text of the decisions, shows why each result matched, and lets you narrow by court, year, outcome, judge, type of case or a case the results must cite. A list of results can be downloaded as a spreadsheet or Word file.
  • The reader. Opening a result shows the decision with headings, numbered paragraphs and quotations set out by fixed rules. Citations and law references are marked in the text; a pinpoint citation can show the paragraph it points to, and a law reference the section text, when they are stored. A side panel lists the case details and every authority the decision relies on.
  • Intelligence and statistics. Judge Profiles, Citation Intelligence and Federal Court Analytics add up many decisions or court records at once, always with the count behind each figure.
  • Workbench and Live analysis. The Workbench keeps the decisions you pin. Live analysis reads a document you drop in, lists the cases and laws it cites and links them to the library. The document is read in memory and not kept.

What I do not claim

  • It is a research aid, not legal advice. Automated reading makes mistakes. Check anything that matters against the source decision, which every result links to.
  • No accuracy figure is promised. Each automated step is tested against decisions I have read myself, and the weak ones are listed above.
  • Public data only. Everything in the library comes from public sources. Files you open in the tools are not stored.
  • One computer. The site and its database run on my own computer, so it can be briefly unavailable while it is updated.
  • Source terms. Some source data comes with its own terms of use, which would need to be confirmed before any wider deployment.
  • How it was made. I built iLit on my own, using AI-assisted development tools to write and check the code. The site itself uses no AI when you use it.

About · where the demo stands on 6 October 2026

iLit: immigration litigation intelligence

iLit is a research tool for Canadian immigration case law that I am building independently, on public decisions only. It lets you search the library, read a decision with its citations and statute references alongside the text, and see how a judge, an authority or the Federal Court docket fits into the wider picture. This page describes what works today and what does not yet.

A guided walk through the real site: it opens each page, explains it and shows its main features. Press Next to go on, or Exit at any time. The only thing it saves is a few example pins on a demo Workbench.

LiveCase search

Search runs entirely on the stored library. Nothing you type is sent to an AI service.

  • Find a case. Type a case name, party, citation or phrase. The search looks at names and citations and at the reasons in the decisions themselves. A short plain-words line under the box says what the search is doing.
  • See why it matched. Each result shows an excerpt with your words highlighted, the court, date, outcome and how many other decisions cite it.
  • Narrow it down. Advanced filters cover court, decision year, decision result, judge, Minister or government party, a tag, and a case that the results must cite. Court and year chips narrow results in one click, and results can be sorted by newest, oldest, relevance or most cited.
  • Take results with you. A Copy citation button sits on results and in the reader, and a search can be downloaded as a spreadsheet or Word file.

LiveThe case reader

Open any result to read the decision in a clean formatted view built from the original text.

  • Readable layout. Headings, numbered paragraphs, quotations and signature blocks are set out by fixed rules rather than shown as one block of text. A header row gives the court, citation, case name, file number, decision date, decision maker and outcome, with links to the source.
  • Citations and statutes in the text. Citations to other cases and references to statutes are highlighted in place. Hovering over a pinpointed citation shows the paragraph it points to, when that case is in the library, and hovering over a statute reference shows the provision text where it is stored. A citation that cannot be matched says so rather than guessing.
  • Around the text. A side panel gives the case information and the authorities and statutes the decision refers to, and there is a search box for the text of the decision itself. A chunk-by-chunk view of the same text is also available.
  • Outcomes say when they are unsure. The outcome shown comes from fixed rules read against the decision. If the rules cannot tell, it says “unclear” instead of picking one.

Some reader tools are still unfinished, so they stay hidden unless you switch on “Show experimental” at the top of the reader.

LiveIntelligence / Statistics

Three views that add up many decisions.

Judge Profiles

A judge’s decisions in the library, outcomes by issue with the full count shown (unclassified decisions included), Federal Court docket activity, and a side-by-side comparison of two judges. Judge names in the source decisions are written many ways, so duplicate profiles still exist while the cleanup is finished. Read these as descriptions, not rankings.

Citation Intelligence

Pick one case and see which decisions cite it, how that has changed over time, the courts and outcomes of the citing decisions, and the passages where it is cited.

Federal Court Analytics

Charts and tables over the Federal Court docket layer: how files progress, leave and judicial review outcomes by year filed, the time between steps, motions by type, judges, counsel and the registry. The docket is typed by Registry staff, so any figure inherits their errors.

How it works, and what I do not claim

  • No AI is needed to use the site. Search, the reader and the statistics read data that was prepared in advance. What you type and any file you open is not sent to any AI service. AI-related features exist in the code but are switched off by default.
  • The original comes first. Each decision is stored as published, and everything shown beside it (outcome, case information, paragraph links, labels) is derived from it by rules and kept as a separate layer. Where a fact cannot be found, the page says so.
  • It is a research aid, not legal advice. Automated reading makes mistakes. Check anything that matters against the source decision, which every result links to.
  • Public data only. Everything in the library comes from public sources. The site currently runs on my own workstation.
  • Source terms. Some source data comes with its own terms of use, which would need to be confirmed before any wider deployment.
Judge statistics are sensitive. Figures about individual judges can be misread, so the judge views show full denominators and avoid ranking.
