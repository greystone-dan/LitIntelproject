Why the Division should back iLit

I have been building iLit on my own, on public decisions only: a research tool that shows how the Federal Court has ruled on immigration issues, which authorities carried the day, and where every Federal Court immigration file stands. This page sets out what else is available to counsel, what iLit adds, what it could be worth, what it would cost, the risks, and what I am asking for. I am not asking to be paid for my time.

The short version

Counsel at the Division can find a decision on CanLII, and CanLII now has a plain-language AI search of its own [1,2]. What I did not find in CanLII, or in any Canadian product I looked at, is analysis of how the Minister has fared on an issue across Federal Court immigration decisions, statistics across every Federal Court immigration file, or a check of the authorities in a draft memo against a stored index. iLit does those three things today, with rules that point back to the source text, not generated answers.

The largest cost I am asking about, about C$20,000 once, is small beside a commercial AI platform for 25 seats (reported C$104K–248K every year) and smaller still beside commissioning a comparable build (about C$1.45M at federal rate-card ceilings) [3,4]. Break-even is measured in hours of counsel time, not years (see the calculator below).

What iLit adds, and what it does not

Works today

Outcomes by issue

Filter decisions to those the Minister won or lost, and see trends by issue. Outcome coding is rule-based and shows its source text. I am still checking its accuracy against a hand-graded set of cases, and some outcomes are known to be wrong, so I would not quote a win rate from it yet.

Works today

Every Federal Court immigration file

The docket history of 316,940 immigration files and 3.59 million entries, drawn from Refugee Law Lab data and the Court’s own site, so statistics can cover files that never produced a decision. The Registry types these entries by hand, so errors in them carry through.

Works today

Citations that go to the paragraph

Citations are matched to the case and, where the pinpoint can be read, to the exact paragraph, shown on click. Citing authorities are profiled over time.

Prototype

Memo citation check

Upload a Word or PDF memo; the citations and statute references the rules recognise are resolved against the library. The file is read in memory and not stored.

Not built

Alerts, treatment flags, ID and IAD decisions

CanLII has alerts today. iLit has no “followed or distinguished” flags yet, and no open source exists for Immigration Division and Immigration Appeal Division decisions. These are on the roadmap page.

Not the purpose

Drafting

iLit does not draft text. Normal search uses no AI, and nothing a person types is sent to an AI service.

Return on investment: a formula, not a promise

I have no measured time savings yet, so I will not state a return as fact. The formula is:

annual value = users × share who use it × hours saved per user per week × working weeks × hourly cost

The defaults below are placeholders I chose. Change any of them. The break-even line shows how many hours of counsel time pay back each funding level.

C$34,500value per year at these inputs
67 hoursof staff time to repay C$5,000 (all users together)
267 hoursof staff time to repay C$20,000 (all users together)

Defaults: 25 users, 40% active, 1 hour a week, 46 weeks, C$75 an hour gives C$34,500 a year. At 0.5 to 3 hours a week the same formula gives about C$17K to C$103K. Hourly cost is my placeholder, not a Division figure.

Compared with

A commercial AI platform for 25 seats: C$104K–248K a year at reported list ranges, with no Federal Court immigration outcome coding that I found [3]. Not a like-for-like alternative.

Compared with

Contracting out a comparable build: about C$0.84M–3.26M, central C$1.45M, then C$220K–290K a year to run, at federal IT rate-card ceilings [4,21]. My judgement of team shape and duration, not a quote.

The first thing worth doing with Division staff is a short timed comparison on real tasks, CanLII alone against CanLII plus iLit. That replaces these placeholders with measured numbers.

Risks and how I would handle them

RiskMitigation
Data residency and security. iLit runs from my workstation today.Public decisions only, nothing internal. The long-term plan is hosting in Canada or inside the Division’s network, and any internal documents would need security and privacy approval first. It is not approved for protected information and I am not claiming it is.
AI at the point of useNone by default. Search, the reader and statistics use no AI service and send no user text anywhere. AI is used only to enrich public decisions when they are added, stored as labelled data. The Directive on Automated Decision-Making would apply if iLit ever fed decisions about people [22].
Licence of the open dataRefugee Law Lab data is licensed CC BY-NC 4.0, with attribution and non-commercial use [17]. iLit is a non-commercial project and credits its sources. A Division deployment should have that reading confirmed by counsel.
Accuracy of coded outcomesRule-based, shows its source, graded against a hand-checked set. Known errors are being fixed. No win rate should be quoted before the check passes.
One developerPlain code, a test suite and written documentation in a public repository, so another developer can take it over. Funding at Level 2 or 3 would help create a second maintainer.
Coverage gapsNo ID or IAD decisions, and no open source for them. The Division’s own repositories could fill this, which is why on-premises hosting matters.

Where it would run: short term and long term

I have researched what is published about Government of Canada requirements. I have not found CBSA-specific policy on any of this and I do not claim one. Where a step depends on CBSA or Shared Services Canada decisions, it says so.

Short term · public case law only

A dedicated private computer, and a site hosted outside CBSA

  • The AI machine: one dedicated, powerful computer I own, running open AI models locally on public decisions only. A standard work-issued laptop with integrated graphics can only run small demonstration models, which is why the machine is a separate item (Levels 2 and 3 above).
  • The site: hosted on an external server in Canada, serving public decisions only. No CBSA data, accounts or network are involved.
  • What it requires: public data and open licences only (the Refugee Law Lab non-commercial licence, with attribution [17]); backups; a domain and tunnel or server. No security authorization is needed for public data (inferred; I found no GC rule that applies to a non-government site holding public data).
  • Estimated cost, my estimate and unquoted: the machine as in Levels 2–3; a managed Canadian-region server in the order of C$100–500 a month; no additional approvals cost.
  • Timeline (my planning guess): about 0 to 6 months.
Long term · CBSA internal use

An internal server or network, with protected data

  • Hosting options: a CBSA or Shared Services Canada data centre, or a Government of Canada approved commercial cloud. The 2017 Direction on the Secure Use of Commercial Cloud Services allows Protected B information in the public cloud [23]. Shared Services Canada brokers cloud services under framework agreements with eight providers [24]. Whether a Division-level tool can use either route is a CBSA decision (unverified).
  • Security: Protected B, medium integrity, medium availability is the usual baseline, using the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security control profile and a cloud provider assessment [25,26]. This involves a security assessment and authorization by the department. I have not priced it; it is CBSA’s process and cost.
  • Privacy: the Directive on Privacy Practices requires a privacy impact assessment when personal information is used in a new or substantially modified program [27]. Internal files will contain personal information.
  • AI use: the Treasury Board guide on generative AI advises engaging legal counsel, privacy and security experts and the Chief Information Officer’s office before deploying such tools [28]. The Directive on Automated Decision-Making applies if outputs feed decisions about people [22].
  • Also needed: accounts and sign-in through CBSA, roles and an audit log, internal repositories connected with approval, and a named owner and support arrangement so it does not depend on one person.
  • Timeline (my planning guess): about 6 to 24 months, mostly governed by approvals.

Phased plan

  1. Phase 1: make it dependable (now to about three months)Finish accuracy checks on outcomes and citations, add the missing court decisions (RAD, newer Federal Court files), schedule daily intake. Funding Level 1.
  2. Phase 2: test it with Division staff (about months 3 to 6)A small group of counsel uses it on real work; I time it against CanLII to replace the placeholders above with measured numbers. Funding Level 2.
  3. Phase 3: a secure home (about months 6 to 12)Hosted in Canada or on the Division’s network, with sign-in and logging, and a local AI model. Only then would internal documents be considered. Funding Level 3, and security review.

The ask

I am not asking for pay or for a procurement. I am asking the Division for three things:

  • Interest and a few hours from a small group of counsel to try iLit on real files and tell me what is missing.
  • An unofficial funding contribution at one of the three levels, starting with Level 1 or 2.
  • A conversation about where it could eventually be hosted, and who would review security and privacy.

If the tool does not save counsel time in the trial, the numbers above will show it and I will say so.
