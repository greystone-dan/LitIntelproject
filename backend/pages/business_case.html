1Executive summary

Purpose

This report makes the case for supporting iLit, a research tool for Canadian immigration case law that I built independently over about two months, using public decisions only. It covers the problem, what exists today, the alternatives, the cost of continuing, the expected value and its limits, the risks, and a staged plan to move from a public prototype to something the Division could use in its own environment.

The problem

As I understand it, the Division’s only research tool is CanLII, which is free, reliable and now offers a plain-language AI search of its own [1,2]. CanLII helps counsel find and read a decision. It does not, as far as I could find, show how the Minister has fared on an issue across Federal Court immigration decisions, give statistics across every Federal Court immigration file, or check the authorities in a draft memo against a stored index. The same is true of the commercial platforms I reviewed (Appendix A). Those answers currently have to be assembled by hand.

The proposal

Continue developing iLit in two stages. In the short term, host the site on a Canadian server instead of my personal laptop and keep building on public case law. In the longer term, and only if the Division wishes, run a version on a dedicated local machine, which is what makes it possible to apply the same features to internal and private documents without that text leaving the machine. Nothing in the first stage touches CBSA systems or data.

Key findings

  • Gap. I found no Canadian product, free or commercial, that offers Federal Court immigration outcome analytics, whole-docket statistics and an authority check on a memo in one place (Appendix A). Whether any vendor offers parts of this under a private quote is unverified.
  • Working evidence. The live site holds 75,677 decisions from the Federal Court, Federal Court of Appeal, Supreme Court, Refugee Protection Division and Refugee Appeal Division, about 1.6 million citations and the docket history of 316,940 Federal Court immigration files, all searchable. Some features are prototypes and some outcome labels are being corrected (sections 4 and 5).
  • Cost. My out-of-pocket spend to date is C$841.32. The funding I would need is about C$1,000 to C$26,000 once, in three levels (section 10). Level 1 is about C$1,000 and covers only the running cost of the services the project uses, paid by the agency directly rather than from my personal card. It does not include payment for my hours. A commercial AI platform for 25 seats is reported at C$104,000 to C$248,000 a year, and commissioning a comparable build at about C$1.45 million (sections 7 and 9).
  • Local AI. The reason for a dedicated machine is to apply iLit’s features to internal or private documents without that text leaving the machine. Public decisions do not need it. I propose releasing the money in three stages tied to evidence (sections 10.4 to 10.8).
  • Return. On illustrative assumptions of one hour a week saved by 40% of 25 users, the value is about C$34,500 a year. No time saving has been measured yet, so I present this as a formula with labelled assumptions, not a forecast (section 11).
  • Timing. Heavily subsidised AI coding plans make this the cheapest moment I expect to build and extend a project like this. That is my opinion and not a forecast I can prove; the evidence and the limits of it are in section 10.9.
  • Safeguards. No AI is used when someone searches or reads. AI is used only to enrich public decisions when they are added. The data licence is non-commercial and is respected (section 12).

2Background and problem statement

2.1Context

Federal Court immigration litigation is high-volume. The Court reported 2,634 immigration applications for leave and for judicial review pending at 31 March 2026 [20]. Counsel preparing a response need to know how the Court has treated an issue, which authorities it relied on, whether those authorities are still good law, and where a given file stands. These questions recur every week.

2.2Current state

I understand the Division has CanLII and no Westlaw or Lexis subscription. CanLII covers all Canadian courts and tribunals, with paragraph-level noteup, alerts, folders and, since March 2026, a plain-language AI search drawing only on its own database [1,2]. It is the correct starting point and iLit does not replace it.

2.3Gaps

  • Outcomes by issue. Finding decisions is possible; learning how often the Minister succeeded on a given issue, and how that has changed, requires reading them.
  • Whole-docket view. Most immigration files never produce a published decision, because leave is usually refused without reasons or the file is discontinued. Tools built on decisions never see them. The Court’s website shows one file at a time and publishes aggregate counts only [20].
  • Memo checking. Checking the authorities in a draft is done one citation at a time.
  • Institutional memory. Positions taken in earlier files are held in documents and in people’s memories, not in anything searchable. Internal documents are outside what iLit holds today (section 9).
  • Hearing-level decisions. I am not aware of any tool, free or commercial, that offers a complete Immigration Division or Immigration Appeal Division dataset. This is a gap across the field, not a feature that iLit lacks relative to others.

3Proposed solution

iLit reads public decisions and court dockets into structured data, connects them to each other, and offers search, a formatted case reader, citation and authority analysis, outcome and docket statistics, and a memo citation check. It is built on open data from the Refugee Law Lab and A2AJ [17,19] and on the Federal Court’s public website.

3.1Design principles

  • Public information only. No internal or protected material is held. Uploaded memos are read in memory and are not stored.
  • No AI at the point of use. Search, the reader and the statistics do not call any AI service, and nothing a user types is sent to one. AI is used only to enrich public decisions when they are added, and its output is stored as labelled data separate from the source text.
  • Rules that can be explained. Case types, outcomes, citations and structure are assigned by deterministic rules that point back to the source text, so a result can be checked and defended, unlike a generated answer.
  • Non-commercial. The Refugee Law Lab data is licensed CC BY-NC 4.0 with attribution [17]. iLit is a non-commercial project and credits its sources.

4.1Case search and filters

Search is where research starts. A plain search for a case name returns the case first, with its court, date, judge, outcome, case type and a count of how many decisions cite it. Filters combine with the words typed: outcome (for example, the Minister won), court, case type, result, cases citing a decision, topic tags found in the decisions, judge, Minister and year. Typing a phrase such as a legal test finds decisions that use it. A plain sentence returns results in a few seconds. Nothing typed is sent to an AI service.

Built but not relied on: a separate Quick Search finds the passage, not the case, that says what a user is looking for (Table 3).

4.2The formatted case reader

Opening a decision shows it as a clean document with numbered paragraphs. The header gives the court, date and citation and counts the decisions that cite it, the decisions it cites and the statutes it refers to. A panel beside the text holds the recorded outcome, case details, case type and topics found in the text. An outline lists the decision’s sections with their paragraph numbers and jumps to each. Citations (marked in gold), statutes (purple) and topic tags (green) can be switched on and off to read the decision clean or annotated. Clicking a citation that points at a paragraph shows that passage in a card, so it can be checked without leaving the page. The Authorities tab lists every case and statute the decision cites, how often and at which paragraphs, with a link that jumps to each place it is cited. The Intelligence tab adds the judge and how the decision has been cited since. Paragraphs that other cases cite are shaded. A decision can be saved to the Workbench.

4.3Citations, authorities and statutes

References such as “2008 SCC 9” or “Vavilov at para 99” are found by pattern, matched to the decision they name when it is in the library, and linked to the paragraph cited. Short forms are traced back to the full case name earlier in the same decision. A citation that cannot be matched says so. For one authority, Citation Intelligence shows which courts and judges rely on it, the outcomes of the cases that cite it and how its use has changed year by year, with the passages behind each year. References to Acts and regulations are matched to a stored library of federal statutes, so a hover can show the provision text. Statute matching is still being improved and a statute link should be treated as a pointer, not a guarantee. Provincial and Quebec statutes are not included.

4.4Case types

Every decision carries a label for the kind of case, for example a judicial review of a refugee decision. It is worked out by fixed rules from the statute provisions the decision discusses, with no AI, so the same decision always gets the same label. Labels are stored for Federal Court, Refugee Protection Division and Supreme Court decisions and can be used as a search filter. Federal Court of Appeal labels are held back because they are not accurate enough yet.

4.5Judge profiles

Every judge in the library is listed with a count of decisions, with the many ways a name is written merged into one. A profile shows the judge’s decisions year by year, how the cases came out and their most cited decisions. Name cleanup is not finished: some profiles are duplicated or missing, Supreme Court panels are not yet split into individual judges, and French names have not been extracted. Because judge figures can be misread, the views show full counts and avoid ranking, and I would use them issue-first.

4.6Federal Court Analytics

This view is built from the Federal Court’s own registry record for immigration files: every application for leave and judicial review as filed, including the many that end without published reasons (leave refused, discontinued, settled). It shows headline figures for whatever the filters select: the number of files, how often leave and judicial review are granted, settlements, how long a leave decision takes, and stays of removal. It shows what happens to the files filed, how many reach a leave decision, a hearing and a judgment, and how each year’s files ended, which no set of published decisions can show. Judges with at least 25 leave decisions appear as points showing how often they grant leave and judicial review; counsel are listed the same way. Filters such as the last five years change every figure, and rates sit beside their counts because small groups are not reliable. The registry is typed by hand, so entry errors carry through to any statistic.

4.7The Workbench

The Workbench is a personal space. A counsel can pin decisions from the reader, organise them in folders with notes, and compare two selected decisions side by side. A case list follows IMM files and a flag shows when the Court’s docket for a file has moved. Today it works as a demonstration: sign-in uses a made-up name with no password to keep lists apart, so it is not a secure multi-user system and holds no real accounts. Real sign-in is part of the internal deployment stage (section 9).

4.8Live Analysis and data safety

Live Analysis reads a document the user brings, such as a memorandum of argument or a decision, against the library. A Word file or a text PDF is read in memory and dropped: nothing is stored, nothing is sent to an AI model or to any service outside iLit, and closing the page leaves no copy. After reading, the page marks every case citation and statute reference in the text, with layers that switch on and off. Each citation is matched to the decision in the library with the paragraphs cited, short forms such as “above at para 99” and “Ibid” are traced back to the case they refer to, and provisions of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act and the Federal Courts Act are tied to the statute library. Cases the library does not hold are still marked, so the user can see what could not be checked. One table lists every authority in the document, how it was written, whether it is in the library and how often it is mentioned. That table downloads as a spreadsheet, and a single action pins all the library cases from the document to the Workbench as a reading list. The de-identifier, a companion tool, replaces names, ID numbers and dates in a document with placeholders. Both tools are prototypes. Checking a Division draft for gaps against peers, and AI analysis of internal documents, are not built; they depend on the internal deployment in section 9.

4.9Keeping the library current

A daily intake job is built. Each run checks the open A2AJ dataset for new Federal Court, Federal Court of Appeal and Supreme Court decisions, adds only the new ones through the same rule-based processing, and reads the Court’s registry for new immigration files and docket entries. It has been run by hand and is not yet scheduled to run automatically.

6Alternatives available to counsel

I reviewed free and commercial resources on 7 October 2026. “Immigration analytics” below means Minister-side outcome coding or Federal Court immigration docket statistics. The full comparison with sources and prices is in Appendix A.

  • CanLII (free) is the benchmark for coverage, alerts and folders, and has plain-language AI search [1,2]. I found no outcome or docket analytics. Whether its AI summaries cover Federal Court immigration decisions is unverified.
  • Westlaw Advantage Canada with CoCounsel and Lexis+ with Protégé and Context are broad research and drafting platforms, reported at roughly US$250 to US$640 per user per month [7,10]. I found no Federal Court immigration outcome coding in either.
  • vLex/Vincent, Clio Work/Jurisage and Alexi are general legal AI tools, hosted commercially [11,12,13,14].
  • Loom Analytics / Court Analytics provides case-law statistics, mainly for civil litigation; its Federal Court immigration coverage is unverified [15].
  • Refugee Law Lab provides free bulk data and a Portal with recognition-rate analytics for IRB decisions and their judicial reviews [17,18]. It is iLit’s data source and a complement, not a competitor.

Finding. Each resource is stronger than iLit in some respect (coverage, drafting, breadth, maintenance). None I found combines the three capabilities in section 2.3. iLit is positioned as a complement to CanLII, not a replacement for any of them.

9Deployment: short term and long term

I have researched what is published about Government of Canada requirements. I did not find CBSA-specific policy on any of this and I do not claim one. Where a step depends on a CBSA or Shared Services Canada decision, it is marked as such.

9.1Short term: external site, public law only

  • Local machine (optional). A dedicated, powerful computer running open AI models locally. It is not needed for public decisions. Its purpose is to apply iLit’s features to internal or private documents without that text leaving the machine. A standard work-issued laptop with integrated graphics can run only small demonstration models, so this is a separate funding item (sections 10.4 to 10.7).
  • Site. Hosted on an external server in Canada rather than my personal laptop, serving public decisions only. No CBSA data, accounts or network are involved.
  • Requirements. Public data and open licences only, attribution to the Refugee Law Lab and A2AJ [17,19], backups, a domain and server. I found no Government of Canada rule that applies to a non-government site holding only public data (inferred).
  • Estimated cost. A managed Canadian server at roughly C$100 to C$500 a month (my estimate, unquoted); Level 1 covers about four months of it at the low end; the local machine as in section 10.
  • Decision for the Division. Whether staff may use an outside site that holds only public case law for work. I have no source on this.

Sections 10.4 to 10.7 set out what that would add. How Level 1 maps to real costs. There are two kinds of cost. Running costs keep the site up: hosting, which is my unquoted estimate of C$100 to C$500 a month, and the domain (C$14.85 once). Development costs keep the project growing: the Claude subscription at C$160 a month, which I need as long as I keep building. I would also like the agency to pay the providers directly, for example through an organizational account or purchase card, so the project does not depend on my personal card.

My estimate of the annual cost to keep going is C$3,000 to C$8,000, in two parts. Running the site costs an external Canadian server at an assumed C$100 to C$500 a month (C$1,200 to C$6,000 a year); this figure is unquoted. Development tools cost the Claude subscription at C$160 a month (C$1,920 a year), which the site itself does not need but I do to keep building. An internal deployment would have its own costs, set by CBSA processes that I have not priced.

10.4What a local AI machine would add

The pitch for the machine is simple: it lets iLit’s features run on the Division’s own internal and private documents, which cannot be sent to a public AI service. Public decisions can already be handled with hosted tools, so the machine is not needed for them. Today, AI enrichment has been run only on 300 core decisions with a small hosted model. A small local model has been wired in and scored 3 out of 10 on a paragraph-summary test, so the local path exists but is not yet good enough [3,37]. A capable machine would let me test and run larger open models on iLit’s own tasks. It does not guarantee they are good enough; that has not been measured. 10.5The design rule stays the same

None of this changes how the site behaves for a user. Searching, reading and the statistics read results stored in advance and call no AI service. AI, local or hosted, is used to enrich public decisions when they are added, and its output is stored as labelled data separate from the source text. The one place a model could ever touch a query is optional search by meaning, which is off today and which would use a local model so that nothing typed leaves the system. Live Analysis reads a document in memory and keeps nothing, and on a CBSA machine nothing would leave CBSA.

10.6Why a local machine

The purpose of a local machine is to apply iLit’s features to internal or private documents without that text leaving the machine. For public decisions alone, hosted tools are enough and Levels 1 and 2 are sufficient. The machine in Level 3 is justified by what only it can do:

  • Confidentiality. Internal documents cannot be sent to a public AI service, so any AI reading of them has to happen locally. This is the central reason.
  • Durability. A local model keeps working whatever happens to hosted providers’ prices or terms, so the features that use it continue.
  • Repeatability. Analysis can be re-run as rules and prompts improve, with no per-use fee and no vendor changing a model underneath it.
  • Residency. Work product stays on a machine in Canada, under my control now and the Division’s later.
  • Portability. The code, the model weights and the evaluation set move with the work, so a CBSA-hosted version could reuse them.

If the Division does not intend to use internal documents, there is little reason to buy the machine.

Alternative: a provided machine instead of a purchase. It may be possible to obtain a high-powered computer through an internal AI program or IT, rather than buying one. This is not confirmed and I have not named a program. If it happens, the cost in Level 3 falls to the support and subscription lines. The conditions I would expect: IT would need to unlock the machine, it would need to run standalone and off the network so that I can keep developing on it, and it would hold public law data only until the Division decides otherwise. I would compare it against the reference configuration above with the same pass marks.

10.9Timing: the current AI market

This section explains why I think the timing matters. Statements of fact carry a source; everything else is my opinion or a forecast and is labelled as such.

What is happening (sourced). On 27 April 2026 GitHub announced that from 1 June 2026 Copilot would replace premium requests with monthly credits that are used up by the tokens a session consumes, at the published rates of each model. It said the old model “is no longer sustainable” because long, multi-step agent sessions now cost far more to run than a quick question. The monthly plan prices did not change; the included credits equal roughly the plan price, and further usage is bought separately [38]. Before the change GitHub had also imposed temporary limits and paused some sign-ups on individual plans [38,39]. A pricing tracker records several plan and price changes at another coding-tool vendor, Cursor, during 2026, though its sources disagree on the details [39]. Taken together, these show coding tools moving from flat monthly pricing towards metered pricing.

What I have had (my own figures). Building iLit cost me C$841.32 to date, of which C$552.23 was a GitHub Copilot subscription with about 20,000 credits a month and no extra usage charges, and the rest was a Claude subscription at C$160 a month plus small API credits (Table 10). For that, I built the library of 75,677 decisions, the docket layer and the tools in section 4. This is a very good return on the money, and I believe it reflects pricing that is unusually generous for the amount of compute used.

My concern (opinion, not a forecast I can prove). Commentators argue that flat subscriptions are priced below what heavy use costs the provider, and that this cannot last [40]. I have not been able to verify any provider’s actual costs, and the direction is not certain: limits have also been raised at times, and model prices have fallen. But if the pricing moves towards metered billing, tighter limits or higher plan prices, then building and maintaining software with AI assistance would cost more than it did for me. I cannot say by how much or when. My working assumption is that the next twelve months are more likely to be more expensive than cheaper, and I treat that as a risk, not a fact.

What this means for the request. The library, the rules, the tests and the documentation already exist and do not have to be bought again. Funding now pays for the remaining work, including accuracy checks, daily intake, hosting and, if wanted, the local machine, while current plans still cover heavy AI-assisted development at modest cost. The local machine also adds durability. A model that runs on the Division’s own machine keeps working if a provider raises its prices, changes its terms or withdraws a service, so the features built on it can continue. This is an advantage of the local route and not a prediction that any provider will fail. It fits the design rule already in place: normal search, the reader and the statistics call no AI service at all, so the core of the site does not depend on any provider. I make no claim that waiting would make the project impossible; only that it would likely cost more to do the same work later.
