iLit

The Immigration Litigation Intelligence Tool (iLIT) is a website built to support the work of the Hearings and Litigation Division of the CBSA. It can best be described as an enhanced version of Canlii, tailored specifically for CBSA immigration litigation work. Each of the 75k decisions in the library has been processed, extracting intelligence from the plain text, and linking it together to provide valuable strategic insights for users. This tool is designed to allow users to do better research, make better arguments, and improve outcomes.

iLit in five questions

Who
This site has been designed for the CBSA Hearings and Litigation Division, although it may be useful for hearings officers, regions, and other partners such as DOJ and IRCC. This is a proof of concept build. The site is not officially affiliated with the Government of Canada in any way.
What
A searchable library of immigration decisions, joined to the Federal Court's record of every immigration file on its docket and to every case and law the decisions cite. Each decision shows its judge, its outcome and its citations. On top of that sit intelligence layers and advanced features, such as live analysis and FCA Activity data.
Where
Currently this is a private website hosted on a personal computer. It uses only published, public information, from the Federal Court, the Federal Court of Appeal, the Supreme Court of Canada, the Immigration and Refugee Board and the Federal Court docket, and every decision links back to its original.
Why
There is an abundance of information available on the internet that has not been collected, processed, and utilized. This website has been built to give the CBSA the tools to conduct better research, make better recommendations, and win more cases.
How
This website contains a library of decision texts. Computer code processes this text, breaking it into paragraphs, extracting citations, judge names, and other useful information, and stores all of this data in a database. Similarly, the FCA records for every IMM Case ever filed have been extracted from the FC website and stored.

What it connects

Three kinds of public information, normally on different sites, joined into one library.

Citations, explained

Every citation is a link with two ends. iLit records both, down to the paragraph or section.

Canadian law is built on precedent: a court follows earlier decisions and the statutes, so every citation is a piece of legal evidence. It shows which authorities a judge found persuasive, which have lasted, and which arguments have worked. Almost none of this is written down in one place. iLit has read it out of 75,677 decisions and stored it, down to the paragraph and section, as a database that can be searched, counted and followed in both directions: from a decision to what it relied on, and from an authority to every decision that has relied on it since.

What it cites
Every case and statute in the decision is marked in the text. Hover a citation to read the paragraph being relied on, or click it to open the case beside the one you are reading.
Who cites it
For any decision, iLit shows the later decisions that cite it, which courts, and how those files ended. That tells you whether an authority is still carrying weight, and for which side.

Where files go

Research sites are built around published decisions. But most Federal Court immigration files never end in one, and leave is usually decided without written reasons. iLit also holds the Federal Court's record of every file on its docket, so it can show the whole picture, not only the files that produced a decision.

How it works

The work is done once, when a decision is added. Using the site only reads what is already stored.

Built to keep growing: the import tools for new decisions and for new FC Activity updates are built. Automatic daily updates are not switched on yet.

What iLit is not

  • Not legal advice. iLit is a research aid. It does not advise and it does not predict results.
  • Not official. iLIT is a proof of concept. It is not affiliated with the Government of Canada in any way and no court has endorsed it.
  • Not always right. Outcomes and labels come from fixed rules and AI models. Where a figure is uncertain, the page says so.
